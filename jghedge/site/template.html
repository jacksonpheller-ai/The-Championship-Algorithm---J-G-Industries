    Games

    We bet one market on games: the total (over or under the combined points), on Fliff. Each row is the side our number favours, with our chance against the chance the price needs. Open Details for how we got the number. Spreads and moneylines are in the details but are not bet: they lost money in two of three replayed seasons.

    Parlays of totals

    Player props

    We bet player props on Fliff as straight bets: over or under a player's line, at Fliff's odds. Our chance comes from DraftKings' price for the same player with its margin taken out, corrected for the one bias we can measure (unders win more often than priced). Fliff keeps a margin of its own, so most props have no edge. A prop is a bet only when Fliff's line or odds are better than the take-it price on its row.

    How to use it

    1. Find the player's prop on Fliff.
    2. Type Fliff's line and its odds for the over, the under, or both, on that player's row here. Odds as Fliff shows them: −115, +105.
    3. The row turns Edge, Close or None. An Edge is added to Tonight's plan with a stake.

    How it works

    The desk does one thing: it finds bets that win more often than their price assumes, and tells you how much of tonight's amount to put on each. Most nights that is very little, and some nights it is nothing.

    1. What happens each day

    The model runs 13 times a day, at set times. 8 am, noon and 2 pm Eastern, then every hour from 4 pm to 1 am. Each run pulls the schedule, the injury report, and DraftKings' lines and player props through ESPN, rebuilds every team and player rating from games already played, simulates every game 20,000 times, prices every bet and grades any finished night. Every run prices tonight's games and, as soon as a sportsbook total is posted for it, tomorrow's slate too, so a new line is seen within about an hour of being posted: the totals edge is at the opening line, before it settles. A separate scheduler starts these runs on time; GitHub's own schedule, which started runs hours late, is kept as a backup.

    The page keeps itself current in between. While it is open it reads the current DraftKings totals straight from ESPN every 90 seconds and re-prices each game in your browser exactly as the model run did: the same blend of our number with the market's, the same error table, the same safety cut and floor. The header line says "lines live" when that is working. A live line can take a bet off tonight's plan; it never adds a stake the model run did not make. Every few minutes the page also asks whether a newer model run exists and loads it in place. Player props are not re-priced live: DraftKings' prop prices change only with a model run, and Fliff's numbers are the ones you type. Games that have tipped drop off by your device's clock.

    2. Where the edge comes from

    • Game totals, on Fliff. Our points projection disagrees with the posted total early enough (before the line settles) to beat the price. This is the edge with the most evidence behind it: the method has been replayed on three past seasons using the lines, results and rosters known before each game. Its settings were fitted on those same seasons and the replay assumes every bet was filled at the opening line, so the replay flatters it. The live record is the real test.
    • Player props, on Fliff. We don't out-predict the sportsbooks on players. We take DraftKings' price as the best guess, correct it for the one bias we can measure (unders win more often than priced), and bet on Fliff only when Fliff's line or odds are better than that fair price. That happens when Fliff is slow to move a line or prices a player differently. Most nights most props have no edge, and this edge has no track record yet.

    Everything else is priced but not bet. Spreads and moneylines lost money in two of three replayed seasons. Our own player projections, tested on a season they hadn't seen, made the sportsbook's price worse, so they are shown for reference and never used.

    3. How a game total is priced

    1. Team ratings. Points scored and allowed per 100 possessions, and pace, from every box score this season, weighted toward recent games and adjusted for opponent and home court.
    2. Who is playing. Each player's value comes from Box Plus/Minus and their individual offensive and defensive ratings. The injury report becomes a chance of playing, and the missing minutes move the team's ratings.
    3. Schedule. Rest, back-to-backs, three games in four nights, travel, time zones and altitude.
    4. Blend with the market. Our total counts for 30% six or more hours before tip, falling to 0% at tip; the rest is the market's number. Late in the season (teams 70+ games in) 1.2 points are added (2.5 in the last few games or with a tanking underdog), the one totals pattern that held on seasons it wasn't fit on.
    5. Simulate. 20,000 games per matchup, using how far real games have landed from the projection, give the chance of the over and the under at the posted line.
    6. Safety cut and the floor. The edge is halved (section 5). A total is a bet when its edge is still at least 1.5% per $1 after the cut.

    4. How a player prop is priced

    1. Each player's minutes and stat line are simulated inside the game simulation (pace, blowouts, who else is out).
    2. That spread of outcomes is re-centred on DraftKings' line and price, so at DraftKings' line our chance starts from the sportsbook's own chance with the bookmaker's margin removed. Our own player projections are not used: tested on a season they hadn't seen, they made the sportsbook's price worse.
    3. The under lean. Sportsbook unders win more often than their price says. On 8,009 past DraftKings lines overs hit 3.4 points less than priced (2.8 in 2025–26 and 4.9 so far in 2026–27; the same direction in every month and every stat), and correcting for it on one season improved the forecasts on the other. So 3.0 points are moved from the over to the under. The figure blends that history with the desk's own log of this season's lines. One exception: where the under is the longshot (DraftKings gives it less than 45%) the lean was not there (+0.6 points on 1,080 lines, against +3.8 on the other 6,657), so none is applied.
    4. Fliff's number. The chance is read at Fliff's line and compared with what Fliff's odds need. The server can't read Fliff's board, so you type Fliff's line and odds on the prop's row. Until you do, the row shows DraftKings' number as a stand-in and the price to look for ("Under 24.5 at −104 or better").
    5. The rule. A prop is a bet when it is worth at least 3% per $1 at Fliff's number, the same bar a game total has to clear. The edge is then cut in half (section 5) and the stake is a quarter of the Kelly stake on what is left. A chance more than 12 points above what the price needs is counted as that many and flagged: a gap that large is usually injury news or a stale line.

    Why most props show no edge. PrizePicks paid one fixed multiple whatever the odds, so a side a sportsbook priced at 60% was a bargain there. Fliff is a sportsbook: a 60% side costs about −170, and Fliff keeps a margin on both sides. On those 8,009 DraftKings lines, betting every under at DraftKings' own odds returned −0.2% per $1 and every over −13.1%. The lean is real, but the bookmaker's margin (6.8% across the two sides at the median) uses it up. An edge is left only where Fliff's line or odds are better than DraftKings' fair price, and the take-it price is that test.

    Evidence for reading a chance at a different line: on 2,866 DraftKings prop lines that moved between open and close, this method (priced at the old line, anchored to the new price) predicted 60.7% for the favoured side, which hit 59.0%. There is no history of Fliff's prop lines, so Fliff props are tracked from scratch on the Record tab.

    What a percentage on a bar means

    A percentage on a bar is a chance, not an edge: how many of 100 bets like it we expect to win. The edge is what is left after the price: every bet has a chance it needs just to break even, and only the part above that is profit. Slide to see what a given chance is worth in each kind of bet.

    5. The safety cut

    Across 821 replayed bets the model expected +9.7% and made +6.6%; on totals it expected +6.3% and made +3.4%. So every edge is cut in half before anything is decided. A total the model gives 56% at −110 (which needs 52.4%) is treated as 54.2%. A prop is cut halfway toward the break-even of Fliff's odds the same way. Every chance on this page is the cut one.

    6. The night's call

    The desk scores the night by the cut expected profit of its own ticket ($100 bankroll, 50¢ bets, a quarter of the Kelly stake). Below 0.05% it is a pass: nothing earns its risk, and the desk stakes nothing on games even if one total still shows an edge (the row says so). The replay backs this: totals bet on the thinnest betting nights (a score of 0.05–0.10%) returned about −2% per $1 over 260 replayed bets (two random seeds), against about +10% on bet nights, so a night thinner still is not worth chasing. From 0.05% to 0.15% it is a light night: thin edges. From 0.15% it is a bet night. The call is about game bets. Player props are sized one at a time (section 7), because each is checked against Fliff's own number.

    7. How your nightly amount is split

    1. Everything is bet on Fliff.
    2. Player props first. The recommendation lists the props closest to an edge as cards: "bet it only if Fliff shows −104 or better". Each prop you have checked against Fliff that has an edge gets a quarter of its Kelly stake, counting your nightly amount as a tenth of your bankroll. A prop worth +7% per $1 at −110 gets about a tenth of the amount; a thinner edge gets less. One prop per player, and props together get at most half of the amount, because there is no record yet of how Fliff props do.
    3. Game bets with the rest. All of it on a bet night, half on a light night, none on a pass. It is split across the night's game bets in the same proportions as the desk's own ticket, so larger and surer edges get more, in steps of Fliff's $0.25 minimum.
    4. You keep whatever isn't used.

    The desk doesn't know your bankroll and doesn't need it, but it can't protect it either. Keep the nightly amount to a small slice of the money you've set aside for this (a tenth or less), so a bad night can't do much damage. Expect losing nights: the edges are a few percent.

    Words on this page

    Our chance
    How often we expect the bet to win, after the safety cut.
    Needs
    How often the bet has to win to break even at its price: 52.4% at −110, 55.6% at −125.
    Edge
    Expected profit per $1, after the cut. +2% means $2 a $100 in the long run, with big swings night to night.
    Our line
    The total at which we think over and under are equally likely.
    Worst line
    The worst number at which a total is still a bet: the highest line for an over, the lowest for an under. Past it, skip.
    Fair chance
    For a prop: DraftKings' price with the bookmaker's margin removed and the under lean applied.
    Take-it price
    The worst Fliff odds at which a prop is still a bet (worth 3% per $1). At those odds or better, bet; at worse odds, skip.
    Stand-in number
    DraftKings' line and odds, shown on a prop until you type Fliff's.
    Bet / Light / Pass
    The night's call (section 6): how much of your amount is bet.

    House rules

    • Bet the number shown or better. If the line has moved past the worst line, skip it.
    • Game lines come from one book. The same bet at a better number on Fliff is free edge.
    • Late scratches change totals. Check the injury report, or wait for the later runs.
    • A night with no bet is not a failure. Passing costs nothing.
    • Only bet money you can afford to lose. Legal only where licensed and for adults of legal age. Help: 1-800-GAMBLER.
